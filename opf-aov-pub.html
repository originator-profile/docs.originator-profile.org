This document provides an architectural overview of the Originator Profile Framework (OPF). In digital media and advertising ecosystems, and in electronic mail, the framework gives a recipient of content two things: rich, verifiable information about the originator of the content, which is the Originator Profile (OP), a set of validated attributes about the originator; and attribution: a verifiable binding of the content to that originator. This document introduces the key concepts, data models, operational processes, and governance model that make the profiles of content originators verifiable. Such a profile is built from originator attributes validated by third parties. The overview also includes a comparison to C2PA, current use cases, and distilled requirements. This document is primarily intended for technical audiences and technically knowledgeable end-users to understand the design of the Originator Profile Framework.

Introduction

These days, we need to judge whether the information we receive is legitimate or not. False information and misinformation spread, primarily through fraudulent websites mimicking legitimate organizations. In the advertisement ecosystem, media inadvertently display fraudulent advertisements on their websites, causing damage to their reputation through association with these ads. Conversely, advertisers unknowingly place their advertisements on fraudulent websites, which damages their brand reputation through association with fraudulent sites. The situation thus harms not only readers but every stakeholder.

Then, what do the well-behaved stakeholders in the ecosystem expect? From the reader's perspective, the media should be what it claims to be, and what it delivers should be reasonably reliable. At the very least, the media must deliver information to the readers accurately, as intended. The reader must be able to verify where the information comes from and understand what kind of source that is. Views on what counts as accurate delivery differ; still, information should reach the reader as the media intended — accurately and in a timely manner. The media, for their part, expect that information is delivered accurately to readers. They also expect that appropriate advertisements are displayed on their websites. Advertisers expect that their advertisements are displayed on appropriate media.

Originator Profile Framework is a set of technology and operational guidelines primarily designed for web media, digital advertisement and email use cases to answer these expectations. In technical terms, the Originator Profile Framework provides attribution, a verifiable binding of content to its originator, together with verifiable profile information about the origin of the content. This profile information is the Originator Profile: attributes validated and provided by multiple third parties and composed into one profile.

A Simple Picture: What Originator Profile Provides

illustrates the capabilities of the [=Originator Profile Framework=] in a simple picture.

First, the [=Originator Profile Framework=] provides rich information about the [=Originator=]: the [=Originator Profile=], from which the framework takes its name (①). It includes not only the basic attributes about the organization but also various attributes validated by multiple third parties that are useful for the [=Recipient=] to assess the [=Content=].

Second, it provides a way to verify the integrity of the [=Content=] and to bind the [=Content=] to its origin, the [=Originator Profile=] (②).

Finally, the [=Recipient=] of the [=Content=] can verify the integrity and authenticity of the [=Content=] using the information provided, and also refer to the [=Originator Profile=] to know about the attributes of the [=Originator=] (③).

The framework is designed to be extensible and adaptable to various use cases; for the [=Originator Profile=], it allows adding [=Validated Attributes=] provided by multiple parties. For [=Content=] integrity, the framework is extensible to accept different types of media in different types of serialization. For example, the [=Web Media Specific Model=] allows for the protection of text fragments and other media within a web page.

Accreditation Body

Accreditation Chain

Content
[=Content=] is information that originates from an [=Originator=].
Content Attestation

Content Validity Period
A [=Content Validity Period=] is a period that a [=Profile Annotation=] can state, for which what it states holds. It has to contain the annotation's [=Signature Validity Period=], and no time is compared with it.
Core Profile
A [=Core Profile=] is the set of essential [=Validated Attributes=] about an [=Originator=], issued by a [=Core Profile Issuer=]; a [=Root Core Profile=] is instead issued by its [=Root Issuer=] about itself, and no other party validates it. It carries the [=Originator Verification Keys=] of that [=Originator=]. A [=Core Profile=] other than a [=Root Core Profile=] is part of an [=Originator Profile=].
Core Profile Issuer
A [=Core Profile Issuer=] is an [=Entity=] that issues a [=Core Profile=] under its [=Core Profile Policy=].
Core Profile Policy
A [=Core Profile Policy=] is a set of policies that govern the issuance and management of [=Core Profiles=].
Identity
The [=Identity=] of an [=Originator=] is the domain name under which the [=Originator=] is known in the framework. The [=Core Profile=] carries it, and it is what the [=Verifier=] compares the [=Presented Identity=] with.
Issuer Pinning Record
An [=Issuer Pinning Record=] is a DNS record set, published by an [=Originator=] in the zone of its [=Identity=], that names the [=Core Profile Issuers=] permitted to issue a [=Core Profile=] for that [=Originator=], or declares that the [=Originator=] has no [=Originator Profile=]. An [=Originator=] whose record names issuers is pinned to those issuers. An [=Originator=] that publishes no such record, or whose zone is unsigned, is unpinned where the [=Use Case Specification=] accepts an unpinned [=Originator=]; a [=Use Case Specification=] may instead require the record, in which case its absence is a failure.
Issuer Accreditation
An [=Issuer Accreditation=] is a [=Profile Annotation=] on an issuer's own [=Core Profile=] that states the issuer's standing in the framework, such as that of a [=Core Profile Issuer=] or a [=Profile Annotation Issuer=]. It is issued by a [=Root Issuer=] or by an [=Accreditation Body=].
Issuer Key Record
An [=Issuer Key Record=] is a DNS record set that lists [=Key Identifiers=], not the keys themselves. An issuer publishes it under its own name, and it names the keys whose signatures on the issuer's documents are to be accepted: the key the issuer signs with, a key it is about to sign with, and a key whose signatures may still be verified.
Key Identifier
A [=Key Identifier=] identifies one of the [=Originator Verification Keys=], or the [=Verification Key=] of an issuer with which the documents it issues are verified. It is derived from the key itself, as a digest of the public key computed as the Core Specification defines, so that the same key has the same [=Key Identifier=] wherever it appears: in a [=Content Attestation=], in a [=Core Profile=], and in the DNS. Two keys are the same key when their [=Key Identifiers=], computed with the same digest algorithm, are equal.
Originator
An [=Originator=] is an [=Entity=] that sends some [=Content=] to [=Recipients=]. In the [=Originator Profile Framework=], an [=Originator=] is also an [=Entity=] that creates the various forms of [=Signed Data from Originator=], such as a [=Content Attestation=]. In the framework every issuer is also an [=Originator=]: its own [=Core Profile=] is about it, and the documents it issues are [=Signed Data from Originator=].
Originator Key Record
An [=Originator Key Record=] is a DNS record set that lists [=Key Identifiers=], not the keys themselves. A [=Core Profile Issuer=] publishes one under its own name for each [=Core Profile=] it issues, and it lists the [=Key Identifiers=] of those [=Originator Verification Keys=] of that [=Core Profile=] that have not been withdrawn; its presence means that the [=Core Profile=] has not been revoked.
Originator Profile
An [=Originator Profile=] is the set of [=Validated Attributes=] about an [=Originator=] that the framework makes verifiable. An [=Originator Profile=] consists of one [=Core Profile=] and one or more [=Profile Annotations=], to which a [=Use Case Specification=] can add a document particular to its use case; the [=Core Profile=] contains the essential information, such as the [=Originator Verification Keys=], while the [=Profile Annotations=] provide additional [=Validated Attributes=] about the [=Originator=].
Originator Verification Key
An [=Originator Verification Key=] is a [=Verification Key=] that the [=Originator=]'s [=Core Profile=] carries and with which [=Signed Data from Originator=] is verified.
Permitted Host Record
A [=Permitted Host Record=] is a DNS record set, published by an [=Originator=] in the zone of its [=Identity=], that permits a host that is not that [=Identity=] and does not lie below it to present the [=Originator Profile=] of that [=Originator=].
Presented Identity
The [=Presented Identity=] is the identity under which the [=Content=] reaches the [=Recipient=], as obtained by the [=Verifier=] from the delivery channel in the way the [=Use Case Specification=] defines; for example the origin of a web page or the domain of the author of a message. It is what the [=Verifier=] compares with the [=Identity=] of the [=Originator=] in the [=Core Profile=].
Profile Annotation
A [=Profile Annotation=] is [=Signed Data=], issued by a [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] under a [=Profile Annotation Policy=], that binds a set of [=Validated Attributes=] to the [=Core Profile=] of an [=Originator=], through the [=Identity=] both are about. Profile Annotation Issuer
A [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] is an [=Entity=] that issues [=Profile Annotations=] of one or more kinds. Its standing as one, and the kinds it may issue, are stated by its [=Issuer Accreditation=].
Profile Annotation Policy
A [=Profile Annotation Policy=] is a set of policies that govern the issuance and management of [=Profile Annotations=].
Recipient
A [=Recipient=] is an [=Entity=] that receives the [=Content=].
Revocation Record
A [=Revocation Record=] is a DNS record set, published under its own name by the issuer of a [=Profile Annotation=], whose presence means that that annotation has been revoked.
Root Core Profile
A [=Root Core Profile=] is a self-signed [=Core Profile=] of a [=Root Issuer=]. Every [=Accreditation Chain=] ends at a [=Root Core Profile=]. A [=Verifier=] holds locally, on behalf of its [=Recipient=], the [=Root Core Profiles=] it accepts, distributed with the verification software; initially there is one.
Root Issuer
A [=Root Issuer=] is the issuer of a [=Root Core Profile=]: a body that governs the framework, which signs each of its [=Root Core Profiles=] with a key that the [=Root Core Profile=] carries.
Signature Validity Period
A [=Signature Validity Period=] is the period for which the signature of a signed document is to be relied on. It is the period that the securing mechanism of the document states for its signature, and it includes its beginning and excludes its end. Every signed document carries one, and it is the only period that the Core Specification compares with a time.
Signed Data from Originator
[=Signed Data from Originator=] is the general form of the signed documents of the framework: a document signed by the party it is attributed to, that is, by an [=Originator=], every issuer being one, and carrying the common properties defined in the Core Specification. The [=Core Profile=], the [=Profile Annotation=], the [=Content Attestation=] and the [=Root Core Profile=] are its kinds; gives the model they derive from.
Signing Key
A [=Signing Key=], also called a private key, is half of an asymmetric cryptography key pair used to create the signature of a piece of [=Signed Data=]. The other half of the key pair is the [=Verification Key=]. A [=Signing Key=] is privately controlled by a specific [=Entity=], the signer, and is used to generate the signature that is part of [=Signed Data=].
Use Case Specification
A [=Use Case Specification=] is a specification of the [=Originator Profile Framework=] for one use case, such as Web Media or Email, that builds on the Core Specification. Validated Attribute
A [=Validated Attribute=] is a piece of information about an [=Originator=] whose truth an issuer has assessed before issuing the document that carries it. A [=Core Profile=] and a [=Profile Annotation=] consist of [=Validated Attributes=]: the [=Core Profile Issuer=] or the [=Profile Annotation Issuer=], respectively, validates them before the issuance.
Verification Key
A [=Verification Key=], also often called a public key, is used to verify the signature of a piece of [=Signed Data=]. It is associated with a specific signer, which privately controls the [=Signing Key=], and is used to [=Verify=] the integrity of the [=Signed Data=].
Verifier
A [=Verifier=] is the software component that verifies the [=Originator Profile=] and the [=Content Attestation=] on behalf of a [=Recipient=]. Where it is placed is defined by the [=Use Case Specification=]; for example a browser extension, a mail client, or a receiving mail server.
Web Media Profile
A [=Web Media Profile=] is a set of [=Validated Attributes=] about an [=Originator=] that publishes [=Content=] on a website. It includes domain-specific [=Validated Attributes=] for the web media, such as editorial policy and contact information. In this use case it is part of the [=Originator Profile=], together with the [=Core Profile=] and the [=Profile Annotations=], and the [=Originator Profile=] ceases to be valid when it does.
Web Media Profile Issuer
A [=Web Media Profile Issuer=] is the role of a [=Core Profile Issuer=] when it issues a [=Web Media Profile=] for an [=Originator=] under [=Web Media Specific Policy=]; it is not a separate [=Entity=]. The [=Web Media Profile=] of an [=Originator=] is issued by the [=Core Profile Issuer=] that issued that [=Originator=]'s [=Core Profile=], as the Web Media Use Case Specification requires. The issuer is responsible for validating the [=Attributes=] included in the profile and ensuring that they accurately represent the [=Originator=].
Web Media Specific Model
A [=Web Media Specific Model=] is a model that extends the [=Base Model=] for Web Media use cases, incorporating domain-specific [=Validated Attributes=] and validation mechanisms.
Web Media Specific Policy
A [=Web Media Specific Policy=] is a set of policies that govern the issuance and management of the [=Web Media Profile=], which a [=Core Profile Issuer=] issues, and of the [=Website Profile=], which the [=Originator=] itself issues.
Website Profile
A [=Website Profile=] is [=Signed Data from Originator=], issued by the [=Originator=] itself, that states the websites on which the [=Originator=] publishes its [=Content=]. The [=Verifier=] needs it to bind a web page to the [=Originator=]. This profile includes information such as the origins of the websites. It is not part of the [=Originator Profile=]: like a [=Content Attestation=], it sits outside it, and it is valid only while the [=Originator Profile=] and the key that signed it are valid.

Architecture of the Originator Profile Framework

The architecture of the [=Originator Profile Framework=] is designed to support a wide range of use cases and applications. It is a modular framework that allows for easy integration with existing systems and services.

In the following sections, we introduce high-level concepts and components of the [=Originator Profile Framework=].

Abstract Model: Signed Data with Policy

In this document, we define [=Signed Data=] and [=Signed Data with Policy=] based on "Cryptographically Signed Data" as discussed in detail in the paper [[A Model For Claim Validation]]. We define [=Core Profile=] and [=Profile Annotation=] based on the "Certificate" concepts presented in the same paper. In the following sections, we will elaborate on definitions and their implications.

The terminology used in the paper [[A Model For Claim Validation]], "verify" and "validate," in particular, is different from the terminology used in this document. In the paper, the author dictates that the difference between these two acts is a lack of context in validation. In this document, we use "verify" to mean the act of checking the integrity of [=Signed Data=] using the [=Verification Key=], and "validate" to mean the act of assessing the truthfulness of a piece of information by directly checking the [=Entity=] itself or consulting highly reliable sources, such as government databases. This distinction is essential in the context of [=Originator Profile Framework=], as it helps clarify the roles and responsibilities of different [=Entities=] involved in the ecosystem. The terms also differ from those of [[vc-data-model-2.0]], where validation is the assurance, by a verifier, that a claim satisfies the verifier's business requirements: here, to [=Validate=] is an act of the issuer before it issues a document, and to [=Verify=] is the act of the [=Verifier=].

Signed Data

In [[A Model For Claim Validation]], "Cryptographically Signed Data" is a conceptual model in which an originator signs a payload and bundles three elements: (1) a verification key (or a reference to it), (2) the payload (the subject being attested), and (3) the digital signature over that key and payload. This structure lets a recipient check source authenticity and payload integrity using the corresponding verification key.

Crucially, "Cryptographically Signed Data" alone does not establish the truth of any claim encoded in the payload; it only confirms the authenticity of the source and the integrity of the data. Determining whether a claim is valid requires additional evidence and a validator-specific validation policy (i.e., criteria/intent), which is distinct from mere cryptographic verification.

In this document, [=Signed Data=] consists of three elements: a [=Verification Key=] or a reference to it, a payload, and a signature. It lets one verify that the signature was created over the payload with the [=Signing Key=] bound to that [=Verification Key=]. This ensures the integrity of the payload, and the [=Verification Key=] identifies the [=Entity=] that signed the data.

Signed Data with Policy

In [=Originator Profile Framework=], all of the data models are based on [=Signed Data=] under a specific policy. We refer to this as [=Signed Data with Policy=]. [=Signed Data with Policy=] can provide a way to express the intent and context of a signature based on predefined and shared policies. [=Signed Data with Policy=] is an abstract concept of digital credentials (e.g., [[vc-data-model-2.0]]) and/or digital certificates (e.g., [[X509V3]]).

In [[A Model For Claim Validation]], the authors detailed the relationship between signed data with policy and the related processes. A "Certificate" is a form of signed data issued by a certifier that (i) asserts the certifier has validated a specific claim and (ii) attests that validation according to the certifier’s certification policy (its criteria). Concretely, it bundles three elements — (1) the certifier’s verification key (or a reference), (2) a payload stating what has been certified, and (3) the certifier’s digital signature over that key and payload. Its signature intends to show not just who issued it, but that the issuer certifies the claim according to its policy; validators can then accept the claim by verifying the certificate and trusting the certifier under their own validation policy.

We avoid using the term "Certificate" or "Credentials" to describe the signed data within the [=Originator Profile Framework=]. The term "Certificate" in digital contexts typically refers to a public key certificate (e.g., [[X509V3]]), and the term "Credentials" often implies a broader set of claims or attributes. But in a non-digital world, many of the concepts around certification and credentials are more fluid and context-dependent. The confusion is particularly true in relation to the specific non-digital activities regarding "certification." Thus, using these terms could lead to misunderstandings about the nature and scope of the signed data being discussed here. What we avoid is the word in our prose, not the securing mechanisms that carry these words in their names: a document of the framework is secured by such a mechanism in the form the Core Specification states, at present that of a Verifiable Credential ().

Limitations of X.509 PKI to Implement Originator Profile Framework

When using a digital signing scheme, there is a need to find the [=Verification Key=] of the signer and ensure that the corresponding [=Signing Key=] belongs to the correct [=Entity=]. To implement this, we typically rely on the X.509 [[X509V3]] public key certificate scheme, which is widely used and well understood. However, the current design of X.509 lacks the flexibility that implementing the [=Originator Profile Framework=] requires. This section discusses the limitations of X.509 as the means of binding a key to an [=Originator=] and of adding attributes to it.

X.509 can still be used where a delivery channel already carries it. In email, the S/MIME signature carries the signer's key in an X.509 certificate, and the Email Specific Model () leaves the signature and the certificate path to the S/MIME implementation and adds a check that the key the certificate carries is one that the [=Core Profile=] lists; why the framework does not embed a certificate in the [=Core Profile=] is in .

  • Flexible addition of Attributes is difficult

    Even where an attribute for an application's purpose can be added to an X.509 certificate, the certificate issuer must validate every attribute the certificate carries; and because all attributes travel in one certificate, a certificate cannot be split for presentation, nor combined with certificates from other issuers.

  • Difficult to introduce an amended issuance policy

    There are use cases where specific communities operate specialized sets of public key certificates for particular purposes, signaling that the public key used for signing satisfies specific policy requirements. To implement such a "virtual list" of certificates that satisfy a community's policy, it may be necessary to establish an entire certificate tree, including the operation of a Certificate Authority (CA). Since operating such a CA involves significant work and complexity, it is often not feasible for smaller communities or specialized use cases.

  • Satisfying certificate status checking scalability requirement is challenging

    Public key certificates require periodic or on-demand validity checks. Protocols like [[OCSP]] and CRL [[RFC5280]] are currently in use. However, even within the scope of public key certificates used by web servers, OCSP fails to meet scalability requirements, and CRLs pose privacy concerns. There are some discussions on improvement, but there are no reasonable solutions yet as discussed in a paper [[SoK-DR]]. A recent profile of OCSP for high-volume environments [[RFC9919]] lowers the cost of answering: responses are produced in advance, carry no nonce, are fetched over HTTP GET so that caches between the client and the responder can hold them, and are kept by the client until their next update. It makes each check cheaper and lets a client reuse an answer; it does not change that the status of each certificate is asked of its issuer, one certificate at a time, which is the part that the scale of the domain targeted by the [=Originator Profile Framework=] strains. These factors contribute to the trend toward shorter certificate lifetimes. Furthermore, in the domain targeted by [=Originator Profile Framework=], considering that a much larger number of [=Entities=] will generate significantly more signed data, which a vastly greater number of clients will verify, the scale involved is expected to be orders of magnitude larger. Therefore, a new approach that does not rely on current mechanisms is necessary.

In [=Originator Profile Framework=], these limitations are addressed by a more flexible and extensible framework.

Architectural Design of Originator Profile Framework

The [=Originator Profile Framework=] provides attribution of content to its originator and, at the same time, addresses the limitations of current digital public key certificate schemes described above with a more flexible and extensible way of carrying validated attributes. All signed data in the framework is [=Signed Data from Originator=], which is a kind of [=Signed Data with Policy=]: it is signed with intent, under the policy of the party that issued it.

The [=Originator Profile Framework=] architecture currently comprises three groups of models: the [=Base Model=], a domain-agnostic model; the [=Jurisdiction Specific Model=], which extends the [=Base Model=] to support specific jurisdictions; and the use case specific models, the [=Web Media Specific Model=] and the Email Specific Model, each of which applies the [=Base Model=] to one way of delivering content. We expect to introduce a similar model for each future application domain. Each model is described in its own section: , , and ; the use case specific models are described in outline, and the [=Use Case Specifications=] define them.

In the following subsections, we outline three key data models, the [=Originator Profile=], [=Signed Data from Originator=] and [=Content Attestation=], which describes in detail.

Originator Profile

In the [=Originator Profile Framework=], information about an [=Originator=] that is to be verifiable takes the form of [=Signed Data from Originator=]: data signed, under a specific policy, by the party it comes from. For a [=Core Profile=], that party is the [=Core Profile Issuer=], which is itself an [=Originator=] in the framework (). The information about an [=Originator=] is expressed as its [=Originator Profile=]. An [=Originator Profile=] consists of one [=Core Profile=], which contains the [=Originator Verification Keys=], and one or more [=Profile Annotations=] that attach [=Validated Attributes=] to the [=Core Profile=]. A [=Use Case Specification=] can add to it a document particular to its use case. The Web Media use case adds the [=Web Media Profile=] (), and the Email use case adds nothing.

That an [=Originator Profile=] carries at least one [=Profile Annotation=] is what a [=Use Case Specification=] asks of whoever assembles the documents, as the Core Specification [[OPF-CORE]] states; a [=Verifier=] does not count the annotations, and an outcome with none in force says so rather than failing.

The [=Core Profile=] is a kind of [=Signed Data from Originator=], which is described in the following section. It carries the [=Originator=]'s [=Verification Keys=] and the essential attributes of the [=Originator Profile=], and a [=Core Profile Issuer=] issues it under its [=Core Profile Policy=].

The [=Profile Annotation=] is a set of additional attributes that are issued by [=Profile Annotation Issuers=] under a signature of their own that is independent of the signature on the [=Core Profile=]. [=Profile Annotation=] is also based on [=Signed Data from Originator=]. A [=Core Profile=] may be bound to one or more [=Profile Annotations=] provided by multiple [=Profile Annotation Issuers=], as specified in the [=Profile Annotation Policy=]. [=Profile Annotation=] is an abstract concept, and various specialized types of [=Profile Annotations=] exist; this is one of the extension points for each application domain.

Signed Data from Originator

[=Signed Data from Originator=] is the essential kind of [=Signed Data=] within the [=Originator Profile Framework=]. It is an abstract data model based on [=Signed Data with Policy=], originated from an [=Originator=], assuming that it is signed with a specific intent corresponding to the policy stated by the [=Originator=]. It is bound to the [=Originator Profile=] of its [=Originator=] by the [=Identity=] of that [=Originator=].

Content Attestation

A [=Content Attestation=] is a type of [=Signed Data from Originator=] that confirms the [=Content=] originated from the corresponding [=Originator=] and also has not been altered. A [=Content Attestation=] can be [=Verify|Verified=] by using the [=Verification Key=] contained in the [=Core Profile=], which is part of the [=Originator Profile=]. The [=Recipient=] can thus verify the integrity of the [=Content=] and, through the [=Originator Profile=] it refers to, learn who the [=Originator=] is and what the [=Originator=] has committed to.

[=Content Attestation=] is provided within or alongside the [=Content=] itself. [=Content Integrity Descriptor=], which is part of the payload of the [=Content Attestation=] provides additional information about the content's integrity. The Web Media Use Case Specification [[OPF-WEB-MEDIA]] is where the [=Content Integrity Descriptor=] for the web media is to be described ().

Base Model

The [=Base Model=] fixes the entities, the data models, the processes and the policies that every use case shares; the use case specific models build on it.

Entities

The following are the key [=Entities=] in the Base Model.

  • [=Originator=]
  • [=Recipient=]
  • [=Core Profile Issuer=]
  • [=Profile Annotation Issuer=]
  • [=Verifier=], the software component that acts for the [=Recipient=]
  • [=Root Issuer=]
  • [=Accreditation Body=]

Data Models

This section describes four data models for the [=Base Model=]: [=Signed Data from Originator=], [=Core Profile=], [=Profile Annotation=], and [=Content Attestation=].

Every document of the framework is [=Signed Data=] secured by a securing mechanism, such as that of Verifiable Credentials [[vc-data-model-2.0]]; the Core Specification states which mechanism the framework uses and in what form.

Signed Data from Originator

[=Signed Data from Originator=] is an abstract data model that represents the [=Signed Data=] used in the [=Originator Profile=]. It includes all the necessary information to [=Verify=] the authenticity and integrity of the [=Signed Data=].

[=Signed Data from Originator=] includes the following properties:

  • Originator — the [=Identity=] of the [=Originator=], by which this [=Signed Data=] is bound to its [=Originator Profile=].
  • Key Identifier — the [=Key Identifier=] of the key that signed it.

In addition to the above, various properties are included as payload for each of the concrete data models.

Core Profile

The [=Core Profile=] is a kind of [=Signed Data from Originator=]. It carries the [=Originator=]'s [=Verification Keys=] and the essential attributes of the [=Originator Profile=], and a [=Core Profile Issuer=] issues it under its [=Core Profile Policy=].

[=Core Profile=] includes the following properties, in addition to the properties in the [=Signed Data from Originator=]:

  • [=Originator Verification Keys=] — a set of [=Verification Keys=] with which the [=Originator=]'s [=Signed Data from Originator=] is verified.
  • Originator Key Record Name — the name of this [=Core Profile=]'s [=Originator Key Record=]: a DNS name, new for each [=Core Profile=] and assigned by the [=Core Profile Issuer=] under its own name, at which the issuer publishes the [=Key Identifiers=] of the keys of this [=Core Profile=] that have not been withdrawn. The [=Verifier=] checks that the name lies below the issuer's node for these records (_originator-key._op.<issuer>), as the Core Specification specifies, and looks it up as it is, without deriving it.

    The name is carried rather than derived so that the specification fixes no rule for deriving the names of an issuer's records and the issuer lays out its zone as it chooses. A [=Verifier=] that built such a name itself could, on any mismatch of normalization, find no record: for an [=Originator Key Record=] it would fail a [=Core Profile=] in force, and for a [=Revocation Record=] it would read a revoked annotation as unrevoked.

Please refer to Originator Profile Blueprints: Core Profile Data Model for current implementation details.

The [=Originator Verification Keys=] are a set. Each key is identified by its [=Key Identifier=], which is derived from the key itself; two keys are the same key when their [=Key Identifiers=], computed with the same digest algorithm, are equal. The [=Core Profile Issuer=] publishes the [=Key Identifiers=] of the keys of the set in the DNS as the [=Originator Key Record=] of that [=Core Profile=], as the next paragraph describes; where this paragraph speaks of a key's record, it means the record of that key in the [=Originator Key Record=] of a [=Core Profile=] that carries it. A [=Core Profile=] is not altered once it has been signed: the set of keys it carries is fixed when it is issued. When the [=Originator=]'s keys change, a new [=Core Profile=] that carries the new set is issued, and the earlier one remains in force until it expires or is revoked. A [=Core Profile=] may carry more than one key, for any reason, such as signing with two algorithms side by side; but one issued to replace a key does not carry the key it replaces, which would otherwise stay usable until the new [=Core Profile=] expired or was revoked. A new key's record is published before the key is used. A key that has been replaced is retired: it is no longer used for new signatures, and its record stays in the DNS until the last [=Core Profile=] that carries it has expired or been revoked, and for a short period after that, which absorbs caching and clock differences; only then may the record be removed. This keeps a key rollover from leaving content unverifiable. A key is withdrawn when its record is removed from every [=Originator Key Record=] that lists it; a compromised key is withdrawn at once.

For each [=Core Profile=] it has issued, the [=Core Profile Issuer=] publishes, under its own DNS name and protected by DNSSEC, the [=Key Identifiers=] of the keys of that [=Core Profile=] that have not been withdrawn: the [=Originator Key Record=] of that [=Core Profile=]. The [=Core Profile=] carries the name of this record as its Originator Key Record Name. The presence of the record means that the [=Core Profile=] has not been revoked and that the keys whose [=Key Identifiers=] it lists are its keys that have not been withdrawn; its absence means that the [=Core Profile=] has been revoked. The registration of the [=Originator=] ends when the issuer removes all of the [=Originator=]'s [=Originator Key Records=]. A key that appears in the [=Core Profile=] but not in its [=Originator Key Record=], or in the record but not in the [=Core Profile=], is not accepted.

Profile Annotation

[=Profile Annotation=] is an abstract model that adds [=Validated Attributes=] to the [=Core Profile=]. [=Profile Annotation=] is based on [=Signed Data from Originator=]. Each kind of [=Profile Annotation=] is issued by the corresponding [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] under a signature of its own, independent of the signature on the [=Core Profile=]; the issuer may be the [=Core Profile Issuer=] itself or another party. A [=Core Profile=] may be bound to one or more [=Profile Annotations=] provided by multiple [=Profile Annotation Issuers=], as specified in the [=Profile Annotation Policy=].

[=Profile Annotation=] includes the following properties:

  • Subject — the [=Identity=] of the [=Originator=] that this annotation annotates. A [=Profile Annotation=] is bound to a [=Core Profile=] by that [=Identity=], not by a reference to a particular [=Core Profile=] document, so that a new [=Core Profile=] can be issued without invalidating the annotations. An [=Originator=] that wants separate sets of annotations for separate purposes uses a separate [=Identity=] for each.
  • Annotation Data — Additional [=Attributes=] or other information describing the annotation, depending on the concrete annotation type.
  • Content Validity Period — optionally, the period for which what the annotation states holds; it has to contain the annotation's [=Signature Validity Period=]. The [=Verifier=] compares only the [=Signature Validity Period=] with the time at which validity is judged, and does not use an annotation whose [=Content Validity Period=] does not contain that [=Signature Validity Period=], as the Core Specification specifies.
  • Revocation Record Name — the name of this annotation's [=Revocation Record=]: a DNS name, assigned by the issuer of the annotation under its own name, at which a record is published if and only if this annotation has been revoked before the end of its [=Signature Validity Period=]. The [=Verifier=] checks that the name lies below the issuer's node for these records (_revocation._op.<issuer>), as the Core Specification specifies, and looks it up under DNSSEC; a Secure denial of existence means the annotation has not been revoked.

Please refer to Originator Profile Blueprints: Profile Annotation Data Model for current implementation details. Please refer to for one of the concrete examples of [=Profile Annotation=]. Also refer to Originator Profile Blueprints: PA Data Models for more information on implementing profile annotations in general.

Content Attestation

[=Content Attestation=] is a specific type of [=Signed Data from Originator=] that focuses on the attribution of [=Content=] to its [=Originator=].

[=Content Attestation=] includes the following properties:

  • Target — a [=Content Integrity Descriptor=] that describes the target item being attested to and the integrity requirements for that item.

[=Content Integrity Descriptor=] is a structured representation of the integrity requirements for a specific type of content. It includes metadata such as content type, content locator, and content hash. Currently, all of the [=Content Integrity Descriptor=] types are designed for Web Media; thus, the Web Media Use Case Specification [[OPF-WEB-MEDIA]] is where they are to be described. That specification does not yet define them, and until it does they remain as the [[Originator Profile Blueprints]] describe them.

Please refer to [[Originator Profile Blueprints]]: Content Attestation Data Model for current implementation details. Also, please refer to [[Originator Profile Blueprints]]: Content Integrity Descriptor for the details on the [=Content Integrity Descriptor=].

Processes

This section describes two processes for the [=Base Model=]: Issuing [=Originator Profile=] and Delivering [=Content=] with [=Content Attestation=], and then what the queries of those processes reveal ().

Issuing Originator Profile

This process involves the following steps:

  1. The [=Originator=] creates a key pair ([=Signing Key=], [=Verification Key=]).
  2. The [=Originator=] applies to a [=Core Profile Issuer=] for a [=Core Profile=]. The application includes the [=Verification Key=] and whatever information the [=Core Profile Policy=] requires.
  3. The [=Core Profile Issuer=] checks that the [=Originator=]'s application meets the requirements in the [=Core Profile Policy=], [=Validates=] the information in it, and issues a [=Core Profile=].
  4. The [=Originator=] requests one or more [=Profile Annotations=] from [=Profile Annotation Issuers=].
  5. Each [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] [=Validates=] the relevant attributes and issues [=Profile Annotations=].
  6. The [=Originator=] collects the [=Core Profile=] and [=Profile Annotations=] to form the [=Originator Profile=].

Delivering Content with Content Attestation

Assuming that the [=Originator=] has already obtained an [=Originator Profile=], this process involves the following steps:

  1. The [=Originator=] prepares the [=Content=] for delivery.
  2. The [=Originator=] sends the attested [=Content=] with the [=Content Attestation=] to the [=Recipient=], together with the [=Originator Profile=], with the [=Core Profiles=] about the [=Originator=] among which the [=Verifier=] chooses the one it verifies (an earlier and a later one can stand side by side), and the chain of [=issuer accreditations=] that leads from its issuers to the [=Root Core Profile=]. Whether these travel with the content or are fetched separately is defined by the [=Use Case Specification=]; in the current use cases they travel with the content, and on the web the [=Verifier=] also obtains them from a well-known resource of the page's origin.
  3. The [=Verifier=] verifies, locally and without any DNS query, the chain of [=issuer accreditations=] from a [=Root Core Profile=] it accepts to the [=Core Profile Issuer=] and each [=Profile Annotation Issuer=], other than the [=Root Issuer=], and then the signatures on the [=Core Profile=], the [=Profile Annotations=] and the [=Content Attestation=], the last with the key the [=Core Profile=] carries for it.
  4. The [=Verifier=] confirms that the [=Presented Identity=] stands to the [=Identity=] of the [=Originator=] in the [=Core Profile=] in the relation the [=Use Case Specification=] admits: the same name, a name below it, or, where the use case provides for it, a host the [=Originator=] has permitted by a [=Permitted Host Record=]. How the [=Presented Identity=] is obtained, and which relation is admitted, is defined by the [=Use Case Specification=].
  5. The [=Verifier=] consults the DNS, under DNSSEC, for the issuers verified in step 3, for the [=Root Issuer=], and, where the use case so provides, for the [=Originator=]'s own zone; it queries no other name. At the issuers' names it confirms that the key with which each issuer signed is still listed in that issuer's [=Issuer Key Record=], that the [=Originator Key Record=] of the [=Core Profile=] is still published, so that the [=Core Profile=] has not been revoked, and lists the key used for the [=Content Attestation=], and that no [=Profile Annotation=] in use has been revoked. These lookups are independent of one another and may be issued in parallel. For these lookups at the issuers' names, a resolution failure is treated as a verification failure, not as an absence, and so is an answer that DNSSEC does not secure. The [=Originator=]'s own zone is treated differently in one respect only. Where the use case has the [=Verifier=] consult that zone, the [=Verifier=] also reads the [=Issuer Pinning Record=] there. A resolution failure there, and an answer that fails validation or whose state is Indeterminate, are failures, as at the issuers' names. If the record names issuers and the issuer of the [=Core Profile=] is not among them, or if the record declares that the [=Originator=] has no [=Originator Profile=], verification fails. Where the use case accepts an unpinned [=Originator=], an absence proven under DNSSEC and an unsigned zone both leave the [=Originator=] unpinned, and the outcome records this; a use case may instead require the record, in which case its absence, like an unsigned zone, is a failure. That query, too, is made only after the chain of the [=Core Profile Issuer=] and the signature on the [=Core Profile=] have been verified, since its name is built from the [=Identity=] the [=Core Profile=] carries.

    The line between leaving an [=Originator=] unpinned and failing follows what an attacker can cause. A proof of absence and an unsigned zone are states an attacker on the path cannot forge, whereas a resolution failure is one it can cause, so a resolution failure is never allowed to leave an [=Originator=] unpinned. At an issuer's names an unsigned answer read as absence would silently turn a revocation into none, so there an unsigned answer is a failure too.

  6. With the key that signed the [=Content Attestation=] confirmed as current in step 5, the [=Verifier=] derives its outcome from the [=Core Profile=] and the valid [=Profile Annotations=]. The time as of which validity is judged is the time of verification unless the [=Use Case Specification=], within what the Core Specification allows, sets another, such as the time of receipt.

    The time as of which validity is judged is always one the delivery channel gives the [=Verifier=], never one the signer wrote, since the signer chooses what it writes. Whatever that time, the checks in the DNS are of the state at the moment of the query, since the DNS answers only for that moment. Content signed with a key stays verifiable for as long as a [=Core Profile=] that carries the key is in force and its [=Originator Key Record=] still lists the key. Showing later that the content was valid at an earlier time rests on a record made by a party that verified it then, which the framework does not define.

There is a cut in this procedure that the Core Specification keeps sharp. Up to the verification of the signatures (steps 3 and 4, the signature on the [=Content Attestation=] included) the [=Verifier=] works on what it already holds and sends nothing: the chain of [=issuer accreditations=], the keys it needs, and the [=Presented Identity=] are all in hand, and no signature needs the DNS to be verified. The first query leaves the [=Verifier=] only after that, and only for a name taken from a document whose signature has been verified. The Core Specification forbids a query before this point for two reasons: a forged document would otherwise make the [=Verifier=] query names of the forger's choosing, and the query itself tells the owner of that name that a verification took place. Past the cut, the queries that follow from verified documents are independent of one another and may be issued at once; the order of the steps is one of dependence, not of time.

In the worked example of the Core Specification a verification issues seven queries beyond the accreditation chains, which a [=Verifier=] may cache per issuer; the bounds of the Core Specification cap a verification at 151 queries with nothing cached, one more where the [=Permitted Host Record=] is consulted, and at 43 once the chains are cached.

What the [=Verifier=] takes from the DNS is never a bare answer: each answer carries a DNSSEC validation state, and that state the [=Verifier=] establishes for itself: either by validating with its own trust anchor, or by relying on a validating resolver reached over a channel that authenticates the resolver and protects the answer, having assured itself, by configuration or by test, that the resolver validates. An indication of validation that arrives unauthenticated, such as the AD bit in a plaintext answer, gives no assurance, and an answer from a resolver that does not validate carries no state at all. Web browsers and mail clients do not usually validate DNSSEC by themselves, so each [=Use Case Specification=] states which of these two routes its [=Verifier=] takes to obtain a validation state.

Privacy

The verification procedure of the Core Specification [[OPF-CORE]] reaches other parties in one way: the DNS queries. What a use case adds by its way of delivering the documents, such as the retrieval of the well-known resource on the web, is stated in that [=Use Case Specification=]. The Core Specification states, in its privacy considerations, what the queries reveal and to whom. In short:

  • The resolver that the [=Verifier=] uses sees the names queried: which issuers' records, and, where the use case consults them, which [=Originator=]'s records. This is the exposure a [=Recipient=] has toward its resolver for any name, and the same measures apply: a resolver it chooses and trusts, an encrypted transport to it, and query name minimisation on that resolver's side.
  • An issuer's authoritative servers see what resolvers forward: queries aggregated per resolver and reduced by caching. The name of each of its records carries a label the issuer assigned, opaque to others and not derived from the [=Originator=]'s name; since the issuer assigned it, the issuer can tell that some verification of a given [=Originator=]'s profile is taking place, at the granularity of a resolver and a time to live, but not which [=Verifier=] or [=Recipient=] asked. Where one issuer issues for every [=Originator=], as in the bootstrap phase (), that view covers all of them; what an issuer may do with it is a matter for the governance of the framework.
  • An observer on the path between a resolver and an issuer's servers sees the issuer's name and an opaque label, not whose record is being checked. The queries for the [=Originator=]'s own records are the exception: they carry its [=Identity=].
  • The [=Originator=] learns nothing from the queries for the issuers' records, since none of them goes to its zone, and the framework's own records are never fetched from the [=Originator=] over HTTPS, which would show each [=Recipient=] to the [=Originator=] whose content it reads. On the web the [=Verifier=] does request the well-known resource of the page's origin, which the Web Media [=Use Case Specification=] requires of every page; that request shows the origin, which already serves the page, that the [=Recipient=] runs a [=Verifier=], as that specification's privacy considerations state.

Policy

In this section, we discuss high-level policies for [=Base Model=] governing the issuance and management of [=Core Profile=] and [=Profile Annotation=]. These are not concrete policies but rather provide design direction that informs the development of specific policies and procedures. A discussion on the overall governance model is provided in .

Root Core Profile and Issuer Accreditation

Every issuer, whether a [=Core Profile Issuer=], a [=Profile Annotation Issuer=], an [=Accreditation Body=] or a [=Root Issuer=], is itself an [=Originator=]: its own [=Core Profile=] is about it, it signs with keys carried in that [=Core Profile=], and the documents it issues are [=Signed Data from Originator=]. The standing of an issuer other than a [=Root Issuer=] is expressed by a [=Profile Annotation=] on its own [=Core Profile=], an [=Issuer Accreditation=], issued by a [=Root Issuer=] or by an [=Accreditation Body=]. The accreditations form an [=Accreditation Chain=] that ends at a [=Root Core Profile=]: a self-signed [=Core Profile=] of a [=Root Issuer=], a body that governs the framework.

The [=Verifier=] holds locally, on behalf of its [=Recipient=], the [=Root Core Profiles=] it accepts, distributed with the verification software; initially there is one. They are held as a set so that a further root can be added later without changing the structure. A [=Root Core Profile=] does not expire but is replaced through that same distribution: the [=Verifier=] does not judge it by its [=Signature Validity Period=], as a DNSSEC validator does not stop validating when a date on its trust anchor passes. Were a [=Root Core Profile=] judged by its dates, a delay in distributing its replacement would fail every verification at once.

The chain of accreditations travels with the content, together with the [=Core Profile=] of each issuer in it. The [=Verifier=] first verifies the whole chain locally, starting from the [=Root Core Profile=], using only what it received. Only for issuers whose place in the chain has been verified, and for the [=Root Issuer=], does it then consult the DNS: to confirm, in the issuer's [=Issuer Key Record=], that the key with which it signed is still listed, and that neither the [=Issuer Accreditation=] nor any annotation in the chain has been revoked. A forged profile therefore never causes a DNS query: the [=Verifier=] takes a name from a [=Core Profile=] or an [=Issuer Accreditation=] only after it has verified that [=Core Profile=] or [=Issuer Accreditation=]. The [=Originator=]'s own records are the exception in form only: their names are built from the [=Identity=] the [=Core Profile=] carries rather than carried in it as names to look up, and the [=Verifier=] queries them, too, only after the chain of the [=Core Profile Issuer=] and the signature on the [=Core Profile=] have been verified. A [=Verifier=] may cache a verified chain per issuer. It does not keep the chain beyond the lifetime of the DNS records it consulted, nor beyond the [=Signature Validity Period=] of any [=Core Profile=] or [=Issuer Accreditation=] in the chain other than the [=Root Core Profile=].

Core Profile Policy

On the issuance of [=Core Profile=], the [=Core Profile Issuer=] [=Validates=] the identity and [=Attributes=] of the [=Originator=] as required. The [=Core Profile Issuer=] is responsible for ensuring that the [=Core Profile=] is issued in accordance with the established policies and procedures.

The required steps for the [=Core Profile Issuer=] to issue a [=Core Profile=] include:

  • Validation of the [=Originator=]'s identity and attributes.
  • Assessment of the [=Originator=]'s compliance with the [=Core Profile Policy=].
  • Issuance of the [=Core Profile=] upon successful validation and assessment.

The [=Core Profile Issuer=] maintains a record of all issued [=Core Profiles=] and their associated [=Originators=]. This record includes details of the validation and assessment processes undertaken, as well as any supporting documentation.

The policy is similar to that of X.509-based Certificate Authority (CA) policies, and a lot can be learned from the experiences of the ecosystem. However, due to the decomposed nature of the [=Core Profile=] and [=Profile Annotations=], additional considerations may be necessary to address the unique aspects of each component.

OP-CIP is currently developing initial [=Core Profile Policy=] for initial deployment alongside other policies.

As a condition of issuing a [=Core Profile=], OP-CIP currently requires the [=Originator=] to commit to the [[The Originator Profile Charter]], so that every [=Originator=] adheres to one set of principles and practices. The condition belongs to the initial deployment phase and may change as the ecosystem matures.

Profile Annotation Policy

When issuing a [=Profile Annotation=], the [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] first [=Validates=] the identity of the [=Originator=] and then [=Validates=] the specific attributes that the annotation will carry. The [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] is responsible for ensuring that the [=Profile Annotation=] is issued in accordance with the established policies and procedures.

  • The [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] [=Validates=] the identity of the [=Originator=] using [=Core Profile=] before issuing the [=Profile Annotation=].
  • The [=Profile Annotation=] includes specific attributes of the [=Originator=] or other information as defined by the established policies and procedures.
  • The [=Profile Annotation=] is issued upon successful validation and assessment of the [=Originator=]'s identity and the attributes concerned for the [=Profile Annotation=].

The details of the policy depend on the specific use cases and domain requirements. Thus, [=Profile Annotation=] is specialized for these purposes, and the policy for the specialized annotation needs to be prepared.

OP-CIP is currently developing an initial [=Profile Annotation Policy=] for initial deployment alongside other policies.

Jurisdiction Specific Model

[=Jurisdiction Specific Models=] address the distinct needs of various jurisdictions. Above all, the models need to capture how jurisdictions differ in their models of entities, for example, but not limited to, in how legal entities are defined. [=Jurisdiction Specific Models=] may define additional attributes or validation mechanisms that are specific to a particular jurisdiction.

While we design the [=Originator Profile Framework=] for global deployment, we are currently focusing on the requirements specific to Japan as we initially deploy the framework within Japan. However, we anticipate introducing similar models for other jurisdictions in the future.

Organization Profile

[=Organization Profile=] is one of the concrete types of [=Profile Annotation=] that provides validated information about the organization behind the [=Originator=].

While we recognize that an abstract data model is necessary to represent the key attributes of organizations across different jurisdictions, the requirements for this model require further analysis among jurisdictions to ensure that all relevant aspects are captured.

The model may include attributes such as:

  • Name
  • Address
  • Contact Information
  • Registration Details

These attributes, registration details in particular, vary considerably between jurisdictions, following the specific requirements of each. It is also essential to note that multilingual support is required for the audience outside of the specific jurisdiction.

Data Model for Japanese Organizations

In Japan, validating that an organization exists typically means checking its registration with the relevant government authorities. Such validation may include checking the organization's registration number, tax identification number, and other official documentation (note: registration number and tax identification number are different). Additionally, organizations will be required to provide proof of their physical presence in Japan, such as a local business address and contact information.

Once the company registration number has been validated and is carried in a verifiable form, it also gives a way to retrieve further details from the relevant government authorities.

Please refer to [[Originator Profile Blueprints]]: Existence Certificate in Japan for current implementation details.

The [=Web Media Specific Model=] applies the [=Base Model=] to [=Content=] delivered as a web page.

The Model

The figure below shows [=Content=] delivered as a web page. The list after it summarizes the model along five axes, as the Web Media Use Case Specification states.

Delivery
The web page carries the [=Content=] with its [=Content Attestation=], together with the [=Originator Profile=], which in this use case includes the [=Web Media Profile=], and the chain of [=issuer accreditations=]; the [=Originator Profile=] may instead be placed only at the well-known resource of the page's origin, which also carries the [=Website Profile=]. Binding
The [=Presented Identity=] is the page's origin. The [=Verifier=] checks that the origin is one the [=Website Profile=] names, that its host is the [=Originator=]'s [=Identity=], lies below it, or is permitted by a [=Permitted Host Record=], and that the page's URL is one the [=Content Attestation=]'s allowedUrl property names. The [=Verifier=] does not accept a location on the strength of the [=Website Profile=] alone: without the host rule, a mis-issued [=Core Profile=] would be enough to present any website as the [=Originator=]'s.
Where and when
The [=Verifier=] runs in the browsing software, for example as a browser extension, or in a service that retrieves the page, and judges validity as of the time of verification. The [=Originator=] is not required to publish an [=Issuer Pinning Record=]. Where the setting supplies no page origin, the [=Verifier=] consults the record; where it supplies one, the [=Verifier=] may choose to. The outcome carries whether the [=Originator=] was pinned, was unpinned, or had its record not consulted.
Result
The outcome follows the rules of the Core Specification; in a setting without a page origin, the [=Verifier=] reports that it did not check the binding. The result is presented to the [=Recipient=] in the browsing software.
Requirements of this use case
The [=Web Media Profile=] is issued, under [=Web Media Specific Policy=], by the [=Core Profile Issuer=] that issued the [=Originator=]'s [=Core Profile=], and is in this use case part of the [=Originator Profile=], which ends with it. A [=Verifier=] checks that the [=Web Media Profile=] it is given was issued and signed by that [=Core Profile Issuer=] and is within its [=Signature Validity Period=] at the time of judgement. What else it does with one is not yet defined in the Web Media Use Case Specification. The requirements on its issuance are stated in the Operational Governance Requirements (to be published). The [=Website Profile=] is issued by the [=Originator=] itself.

The Web Media Use Case Specification [[OPF-WEB-MEDIA]] defines each of these.

Email Specific Model

In this section, we describe the Email Specific Model, which applies the [=Base Model=] to email signed with S/MIME [[RFC8551]]. The model adds nothing to the DNS design of the [=Base Model=]; what it adds is a binding between the sending domain and the [=Originator=], and rules for where and when the message is verified.

Entities

The [=Originator=] is the organization that sends the message. The [=Verifier=] is the mail client; verification on receipt by a receiving mail server (MTA or MDA) is not defined in this version of the Email Use Case Specification and is left for future work; the receiving mail server that the definition of [=Verifier=] gives as an example is that placement. A mail client evaluates DMARC [[RFC9989]] itself, under strict alignment: where its own receiving server has recorded a DMARC failure, the client respects it, and a pass recorded there does not replace its own evaluation.

The Model

The list below summarizes the model along five axes, as the Email Use Case Specification states.

Delivery

The [=Originator Profile=] and the chain of [=issuer accreditations=] are carried as one part of the signed MIME structure, which the S/MIME signature covers together with the body. The message carries no [=Content Attestation=]: the S/MIME signature over the body and the profile part stands for it, as the Email Use Case Specification states, and the signing key corresponds to one of the [=Originator Verification Keys=] in the [=Core Profile=].

The S/MIME implementation verifies the signature and validates the certificate path of the signer's X.509 certificate as S/MIME requires. What the framework adds is the check that the signing key is one of the [=Originator=]'s, in which the [=Verifier=] matches the key that certificate carries against the [=Core Profile=] by its [=Key Identifier=], and, through that key, the [=Originator Profile=]: the [=Identity=] and the [=Validated Attributes=] the recipient is shown.

Binding
The From domain is the [=Presented Identity=] and has to be the [=Originator=]'s [=Identity=] or lie below it; the [=Permitted Host Record=] is not used. A message that is not aligned under DMARC through a DKIM signature whose d= domain is the From domain is not evaluated.
Where and when
The mail client judges as of display, or as of the receipt it recorded. It verifies the S/MIME signature, then the [=Originator Profile=] locally, then evaluates DMARC, which queries the DKIM key and the DMARC policy record, and consults the records of the framework in the DNS, where the [=Originator=]'s zone has to be signed with DNSSEC and to carry an [=Issuer Pinning Record=]; without them, verification fails.
Result
The result is none for a message that was not evaluated, distinct from a failure, or pass, or fail, and the mail client presents it with that distinction kept; verification on receipt by a receiving mail server, and the recording of its result, are left for future work. A client that does not support the framework treats the profile part as an attachment, and the S/MIME signature still verifies.
Requirements of this use case
The sending domain is signed with DNSSEC and publishes a DMARC policy record that requests strict alignment; the message is aligned under DMARC through a DKIM signature of that domain, with the names of that DKIM key and of that DMARC record resolving as authenticated data; and the [=Originator=] publishes, in the zone of its [=Identity=], an [=Issuer Pinning Record=] naming the [=Core Profile Issuers=] permitted to issue for it. The last gives the [=Originator=] a means, independent of its [=Core Profile Issuer=], to stop its profile from being used in email.

The Email Use Case Specification [[OPF-EMAIL]] defines each of these.

Governance

The architectural design of the data models enables [=Originator Profile Framework=] to provide a flexible governance model that accommodates various use cases and stakeholder requirements; in other words, [=Originator Profile Framework=] is designed to support the diverse needs of various stakeholders involved in the content ecosystem and beyond.

In the framework, the information about the [=Originator=] — its [=Verification Keys=] and [=Validated Attributes=] — is unbundled into the [=Core Profile=] and multiple [=Profile Annotations=], to allow later flexible composition. Each of the [=Validated Attributes=] may be bound to specific policies. These policies and the issuance operation are governed appropriately by the [=Root Issuers=], the bodies that govern the framework.

In the following sections, we first introduce a generic governance model, then show the X.509 PKI-based system as an example of the governance model. Then, we provide examples of governance models for various use cases, including the minimal governance model for the [=Originator Profile Framework=] planned in Japan. We extend the discussion to governance models for other jurisdictions and, finally, for the global community. Each of the extensions requires increasing stakeholder involvement to govern the system effectively.

The technical design places requirements on the operation of the framework, which the parties that operate it are required to meet and which each [=Root Issuer=] imposes: how its [=Root Core Profiles=] are distributed to [=Verifiers=] and replaced; how a [=Core Profile Issuer=] operates the [=Originator Key Records=] of its [=Originators=], including a bounded delay within which a compromised key is withdrawn; how the issuer of a [=Profile Annotation=] operates its [=Revocation Records=]; and, as a condition of a possible later simplification of the verification procedure, that the revocation of an issuer's [=Issuer Accreditation=] and the removal of that issuer's own [=Originator Key Records=], published by the issuer of its [=Core Profile=], take effect together. These requirements are stated in the Operational Governance Requirements, and the values, such as that delay, and the procedures by which they are met are carried in the Operations document (). Neither document is published with this one; until they are, the recommended values that concern a use case are stated in its [=Use Case Specification=], and those that concern the issuers' zones, where one has been set, in the Internet-Draft that defines the records.

A Generic Governance Model

The figure below shows a highly simplified generic governance model distilled from the discussion in the [[Trusted Web White Paper 3.0]]. (Note: the governance model discussed in the white paper is based on the discussion by one of the authors of this document, in the paper [[A Study on Governance funded by JFSA]], which includes an analysis of [[ICANN]] multistakeholder model)

The governance in the digital ecosystem usually revolves around "Data Under Governance." The "Governing Body" is controlling the "Operator." The "Operator" manages the "Data Under Governance." The "Operator" is responsible for overseeing the management and protection of this data under the "Policy," which includes operational policy. The "Operator" operates under the policy faithfully, and the "Governing Body" is able to control "Data Under Governance" through the "Policy."

X.509 PKI based system with the Generic Governance Model

Following is a simplified representation of the governance model for an X.509 PKI system, based on the Generic model discussed in the previous section.

This figure presents a simplified governance structure of the X.509 Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) system. At the top of the hierarchy is the governing body, which establishes the overarching policies and trust framework for the PKI ecosystem. The trust list, together with the governing body, is the root of trust for the community that relies on it.

Under the governing body, Certificate Authorities (CAs) are responsible for issuing and managing digital certificates in accordance with these policies. The CAs act as trusted third parties, validating the identities of entities requesting certificates and ensuring compliance with governance requirements. Relying parties, such as users, applications, or organizations, depend on the trustworthiness of the CAs and the integrity of the certificates they issue to authenticate identities and secure communications. The figure illustrates the flow of trust, policy enforcement, and operational responsibilities among these entities, clarifying how governance is maintained and how trust is propagated within the X.509 PKI environment.

The verifying entity in the figure, which corresponds to the [=Verifier=] in this document, refers to the "Trust List" via the operating system or application, which includes the list of trusted root CAs. The operating system or application vendor typically manages the trust list, and it is updated through a software update mechanism.

From the verifying entity's point of view, the trust list is usually invisible: the operating system or application trusts the root CAs in the list automatically. And because governance is bound to the whole trust tree, the only policies a verifying entity can rely on are those set by the bodies that maintain the trust list and by each CA.

Governance Model for Global Community

The following is a simplified representation of the Originator Profile Governance Model planned for Global Community.

In this model, multiple [=Core Profile Issuers=] exist, each responsible for issuing [=Core Profiles=] to [=Originators=] and operating under its own governance. A lot can be learned from the experience of [[ICANN]]. Comparison to related works or projects

Comparison to C2PA

Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), whose technology is presented to users as Content Credentials [[C2PA-SPEC]], is a consortium of leading media and technology companies collaborating to establish an open standard for content provenance and authenticity. The goal of C2PA is to provide a framework for creating, sharing, and verifying content authenticity metadata, enabling users to trust the content they consume.

The project primarily focuses on addressing the challenges of content provenance and authenticity in digital media, including ensuring the integrity of media files and providing verifiable information about their origins. Thus, the scope of C2PA overlaps with the [=Originator Profile Framework=] in its goals and objectives. We therefore hope to collaborate and align our efforts to achieve common objectives.

That said, there are some key differences between C2PA and the [=Originator Profile Framework=].

  • C2PA binds provenance to an asset, such as a file, while the [=Originator Profile Framework=] binds attribution to a page or a message as delivered. The difference is the unit of the binding rather than the kind of media: a C2PA manifest can be embedded in images, video and audio, in documents such as PDF, and, in recent versions of the specification, in text and HTML documents [[C2PA-SPEC]].
  • C2PA provides historical data: the history of an asset, the ingredients and actions that produced it, together with its signer. The [=Originator Profile Framework=] primarily states who the [=Originator=] is, through attributes validated by third parties, for verification by the end-user. The history is essential for some uses, such as the internal work of web media, but it is more than an end-user usually needs.

    Some members of OP-CIP have an interest in providing content archives, and that naturally may include historical information. However, it will be implemented differently — probably, outside of the media data itself.

  • [=Originator Profile Framework=] aims to provide rich information about the [=Originator=] through multiple [=Validated Attribute=] providers, accommodating various use cases and stakeholder requirements within a flexible framework.
  • C2PA's trust model is based on X.509 PKI: only X.509 certificates are used for signing [[C2PA-SPEC]]. Thus it shares the same issues we discussed in
  • C2PA's trust model rests on trust lists: the C2PA Trust List, a list of certification authorities maintained under the C2PA Conformance Program since mid-2025 [[C2PA-CONFORMANCE]], and, in the news domain, lists such as the IPTC Origin Verified News Publishers List [[IPTC-OVNPL]]. The [=Originator Profile Framework=] also starts from a root that the [=Verifier=] holds, the [=Root Core Profile=] (), but that root is to be governed in a multistakeholder model like that of the DNS under [[ICANN]] (), while each [=Core Profile Issuer=] and [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] sets its own policies, within the standing its [=Issuer Accreditation=] grants, and publishes its own records under its own name, and an [=Originator=] names in its own zone the [=Core Profile Issuers=] permitted to issue for it (the [=Issuer Pinning Record=]), so the arrangement is distributed rather than fully centralized. Establishing a trust list for a specific use case that meets the requirements of a new ecosystem remains a challenge in either model.

Internationalization and Multilingual Support

There are some internationalization and multilingual challenges in the use of [[vc-data-model-2.0]]. The current version of the data model of the [=Originator Profile Framework=] uses a single language for each of the signed data objects. This decision is due to the fact that the current JSON-LD-based multilingual structure is complex to decode on the verifier side, which, in our case, is within a browser. The cost is not negligible; thus, we currently apply one language to the entire [=Profile Annotation=] model, which potentially contains multilingual strings. While it is possible to provide multiple [=Profile Annotations=] of the same type with different languages, the current browser extension does not yet support this. The [[Originator Profile Blueprints]] describe how such annotations are distributed; whether documents that differ only in language are one document, of which a [=Verifier=] uses one, or several, is an open question of the Web Media Use Case Specification [[OPF-WEB-MEDIA]].

Also note that, while the contents of the Web Media use case usually do not require extensive multilingual support since the contents are typically in one language per page, the information within the [=Originator Profile=] requires multilingual support regardless of the contents.

The other challenge is selecting the languages to be included. For Japanese, inclusion of both Japanese and English is necessary, but it is not clear for other languages.

We are exploring various options to address this issue and may propose a different scheme that is more suitable for our use case.

Key delivery and discovery

Earlier versions of the data model embedded a single verification key in the [=Core Profile=] and had no key discovery mechanism, which in effect ruled out key rollover. The framework now treats the keys in a [=Core Profile=] as a set, fixed when that [=Core Profile=] is issued, each key identified by its [=Key Identifier=]; a change of keys is made by issuing a new [=Core Profile=]. The [=Core Profile Issuer=] publishes in the DNS, under DNSSEC, for each [=Core Profile=] it has issued, the [=Key Identifiers=] of the keys of that [=Core Profile=] that have not been withdrawn (see and ).

The framework uses the DNS, under DNSSEC, for three aims: to lower the hurdles of exchanging keys and of managing their revocation, as the list below details; to let each issuer publish under its own name, in its own zone, so that the publication is distributed among the issuers as the governance is intended to be (); and to stay consistent with email, whose senders already publish their keys and policies in the DNS. DNSSEC is needed on the issuers' side, where the parties are few and able to operate it; the [=Base Model=] does not ask it of the [=Originator=], and the [=Recipient=] does not operate it: a [=Verifier=] that does not validate by itself relies on a validating resolver reached over an authenticated channel, as each [=Use Case Specification=] states (). Signing a zone is asked of the issuers, and of an [=Originator=] only where a use case requires it, as email does.

DNSSEC was chosen because it provides, without additional machinery:

  1. Several keys of one [=Core Profile=] coexisting in one record set, each removable independently of the others.
  2. Parallel publication of the records of an earlier and a later [=Core Profile=] for a period, while both are in force, so that a key rollover is a matter of adding a record set under a new name and, later, removing the old one.
  3. Revocation by removal for keys and [=Core Profiles=], and by presence for annotations: a key is withdrawn, a [=Core Profile=] is revoked, and a registration is ended, by deleting the records concerned, while a [=Profile Annotation=] is revoked by publishing its [=Revocation Record=]. The polarities differ for two reasons. The record of a [=Core Profile=] carries the set of its keys, from which a withdrawn key is removed, so it can only be a record that is present, whereas an annotation has no keys of its own and a record for it could carry nothing but its existence. And annotations are many, so a record for each would swell the issuer's zone, while the ordinary end of an annotation is its expiry and only a revocation within its [=Signature Validity Period=] has to be published. Either way the state is a presence or an absence that DNSSEC proves to the [=Verifier=].

The records are published under the issuer's own DNS name, not the [=Originator=]'s, so that an [=Originator=] does not need to operate DNSSEC to take part. The framework defines two records for the [=Originator=]'s zone: the [=Issuer Pinning Record=], by which the [=Originator=] names the [=Core Profile Issuers=] that may issue for it or declares that it has no [=Originator Profile=], and the [=Permitted Host Record=]. Declaring that it has no [=Originator Profile=] is a means by which an [=Originator=] can stop its profiles without its [=Core Profile Issuer=]'s help, as when its key leaks; the declaration stops every one of them at once. Whether a use case requires an [=Originator=] to publish them, and whether its [=Verifier=] consults them, is stated by the [=Use Case Specification=] of that use case; an [=Originator=] whose zone is signed is encouraged to publish an [=Issuer Pinning Record=].

The DNS carries, as a rule, the [=Key Identifiers=] of the keys rather than the keys themselves, which keeps the records small regardless of the key algorithm. The record type that carries the keys also admits a form that carries the full public key instead of its digest (Matching Type 0); the [=Originator Profile Framework=] does not use that form: an issuer does not publish such a record, and a [=Verifier=] ignores any that it meets. The key itself is always obtained from the [=Core Profile=]. The record type is defined in the Internet-Draft draft-shigeya-dns-opkey so that it can be registered with IANA and supported by DNS software independently of the framework.

The framework does not take the following path; this paragraph records why. One could embed an X.509 [[X509V3]] certificate in the [=Core Profile=] and annotate the [=Core Profile=] with multiple [=Profile Annotations=]. But if an X.509 certificate is used, it not only complicates the key rollover process but also forgoes the benefits of DNSSEC, especially the low-overhead revocation.

A [=Core Profile=] can have a [=Signature Validity Period=] of the order of a year, since its revocation does not wait for that period to end: a [=Verifier=] consults the issuer's records at every verification and treats an answer it cannot validate as a failure. A withdrawn key or a revoked [=Core Profile=] is therefore seen within the time to live of its [=Originator Key Record=]. Shorter lifetimes are how the Web PKI compensates for revocation checks that relying parties cannot depend on; the framework needs no such compensation.

Identity Is a Domain Name

The [=Identity=] of an [=Originator=] is a DNS name, so it follows the domain name when the name changes hands, lapses or is taken over. A new holder of the name does not hold the keys of the earlier holder's [=Core Profile=], and so cannot sign with it. The earlier holder, however, keeps those keys until that [=Core Profile=] expires or is revoked. What those keys can still do is limited by the binding of each use case: content counts for the [=Originator=] only where the [=Presented Identity=], the page's origin or the domain of the From header field, is that name or lies below it, which the earlier holder no longer controls. What remains is content shown without a [=Presented Identity=], such as content read from an archive, which the security considerations of the Core Specification treat under pinning. Where the use case consults it, the new holder can publish in the zone of the name an [=Issuer Pinning Record=] that names other [=Core Profile Issuers=] or declares that the [=Originator=] has no [=Originator Profile=], and the [=Core Profile Issuer=] can revoke a [=Core Profile=] whose [=Originator=] no longer holds the name. How an issuer confirms that an [=Originator=] still holds its name is a matter of the issuer's [=Core Profile Policy=].

Profile Delivery/Retrieval

How the [=Originator Profile=] reaches the [=Recipient=] is defined by each use case, not by the framework as a whole. In the current use cases the [=Core Profile=], the [=Profile Annotations=] and the chain of [=issuer accreditations=] travel with the [=Content=]: on the web, in the page and at a well-known resource of the page's origin; in email, as a part of the signed message. The DNS is not used to distribute any of them. Its role is to say whether what the [=Verifier=] already holds is still current: whether a key is still valid, a [=Core Profile=] still in force, an annotation still unrevoked.

Carrying the documents with the content keeps what the issuers see to what resolvers forward: retrieving a document from its issuer over HTTPS would show each [=Recipient=] to that issuer.

A use case may later choose to retrieve some part of what travels with the content, for instance the chain of [=issuer accreditations=], from the issuer's DNS or another channel instead of carrying it with every item of content. Such a change adds a route rather than removing one: a [=Verifier=] ignores any part of what it receives that it does not need, so the addition does not affect existing [=Verifiers=].

Extension of Profile Annotation

One of the key features of the [=Originator Profile Framework=] is its extensibility, which is achieved through the use of [=Profile Annotations=]. Each [=Profile Annotation=] can be defined to accommodate specific use cases and requirements, allowing for a flexible and adaptable framework. The following are some potential uses of the [=Profile Annotation=].

Profile Annotation Issuer Recognition

A kind of membership annotation is an example of [=Profile Annotation=] extensibility.

Interestingly, the existence of a [=Profile Annotation=] issued by the specific [=Profile Annotation Issuer=] itself, without additional [=Validated Attributes=] under the [=Profile Annotation Policy=], is useful enough to show the [=Profile Annotation Issuer=]'s intent. For example, if a media association issues a membership [=Profile Annotation=] to a media company, the existence of the membership [=Profile Annotation=] itself is sufficient to indicate that the media company is a member of the association, even if the [=Profile Annotation=] does not include additional [=Validated Attributes=].

This approach allows for a lightweight and efficient way to recognize the affiliation or membership of an [=Originator=] without the need for extensive data within the [=Profile Annotation=].

This concept can be applied to various use cases that require creating a kind of list of [=Originators=] who meet specific criteria, without creating and managing a list as a database.

Vocabularies and Profile Annotations

Vocabularies will play a crucial role in defining the semantics of [=Profile Annotations=]. They provide a standardized way to represent and communicate specific concepts, making it easier for different systems to understand and process the information contained within a [=Profile Annotation=]. By leveraging existing vocabularies or creating new ones, we can enhance the expressiveness and interoperability of [=Profile Annotations=].

Multiple existing vocabularies and schemas exist and can be utilized for [=Profile Annotations=]. These vocabularies provide a rich set of terms and definitions that can be used to describe various aspects of an [=Originator=] or its attributes.

Paul is an avid reader of news articles, usually reading articles from his favorite newspapers on their websites. Newspaper websites are curated as a whole, not just individual articles, making it easy to access articles Paul wants to read at any given time. Recently, a newspaper company announced a change in its editorial policy, and Paul wanted to check the company's current policy. With OP, he can easily and reliably access information about the newspaper company's code of conduct. Media operational guidelines provided through OP are available through the same interface regardless of the site, making it easy to access the information and contact the relevant parties if necessary.

Nancy: A user who reads news articles via a portal site

Nancy is a user who wants to spend as little time as possible using a computer. She can confirm the relationship between the original article and the summary, who is responsible for the summary, and whether the original article includes a link that has been altered without permission.
